Vertical jump training

How to jump higher for basketball

By Bounce Method · 10 min read

Jumping higher isn't about one magic exercise. Your vertical comes down to how much force you can put into the floor, how fast you can apply it, how springy your tendons are, and how well you use your approach and arms. Train all of those together and your vert goes up. Skip one and you hit a ceiling.

This guide breaks down exactly what to train, a simple week you can follow, how long it takes, and the mistakes that keep most hoopers stuck.

What decides how high you jump

Every jump is the same basic move: you push into the floor and the floor pushes you up. How high you go depends on how hard you push and how quickly you do it, because on a real basketball jump your feet are only on the ground for a split second.

That's why squatting heavy alone won't turn you into a dunker, and doing hundreds of jumps alone won't either. Strong legs give you the force. Power and elasticity let you use that force fast. Speed and technique decide how much of it actually goes into the jump.

The 5 things to train

1. Strength: build the force

Strength is your foundation. The more force your legs and hips can produce, the more you have to work with when you jump. For most young players this is the fastest way to add inches early on.

  • Squats: start with goblet squats (holding one dumbbell at your chest) before moving to a barbell.
  • Split squats or lunges: one leg at a time, for balance and for single-leg jumps off the dribble.
  • Romanian deadlifts (RDLs): for your hamstrings and glutes, the muscles that drive hip extension.
  • Calf raises: slow and controlled, for stronger ankles.

Do 3 to 4 sets of 5 to 8 reps with a weight you can move with perfect form. Strength training is safe for teens when the technique is right and the weight goes up gradually. If you're new to lifting, learn the movements from a qualified coach before adding load.

2. Power: use it fast

Power is strength applied quickly. These are short, explosive efforts done fresh, with full rest between sets.

  • Box jumps: jump up, land softly, and step down. The box is there to save your joints from hard landings, not to test how high you can tuck your knees.
  • Broad jumps: jump forward as far as you can and stick the landing.
  • Medicine ball throws: overhead or scoop throws teach your hips to fire hard.

Keep it to 3 to 5 sets of 3 to 5 reps. Every rep should be max effort. When your jumps start getting lower, you're done for the day.

3. Elasticity: get springy

Your Achilles tendon and the tissues around your ankles act like springs. The stiffer and more reactive they are, the less time you spend on the ground and the more "bounce" you get back.

  • Pogo hops: small, quick hops on the balls of your feet with stiff ankles. Think "the ground is hot."
  • Skips for height: big, rhythmic skips that drive your knee up.
  • Line hops: quick hops forward and back or side to side over a line.

Save advanced drills like depth jumps (stepping off a box and exploding up the moment you land) until you've built a strength base and can land quietly with your knees tracking over your toes.

4. Speed: a faster approach

A running jump is usually higher than a standing one because you carry speed into it. But you can only use the speed you can control. Short sprints of 10 to 20 meters with full rest teach you to accelerate, and practicing your approach at game speed teaches your body to turn that speed into height.

5. Jump mechanics: don't waste anything

Plenty of athletes have the strength and speed but lose inches to sloppy technique.

  • The last two steps: your second-to-last step should be a little longer and lower, to set your hips up for the takeoff. The final step is quick.
  • Arm swing: swing your arms back hard as you plant, then drive them up through the jump. A good arm swing adds real height.
  • Hips back, then up: load your hips like a spring instead of just bending your knees.
  • One foot or two: most players are naturally better at one. Train both, but get really good at the one you use in games.

Film yourself from the side on your phone. You'll spot problems in seconds that you'd never feel.

The key: train all five together. Strength without power is slow, power without strength runs out fast, and neither matters much if your approach leaks energy.

A simple week you can follow

Here's a week built around those five qualities. It's the same structure the Bounce Method program uses: four training days, one lighter recovery day, and two full rest days.

TueTendon, ankle + balancePogo hops, single-leg balance work, light calf work
WedElasticity + speedSkips, line hops, short sprints, approach jumps
ThuRestRecover and grow
FriPower + max jumpBox jumps, broad jumps, med ball throws, a few max approach jumps
SatDurability resetLight mobility and stability work
SunRestGet ready for the next week

Playing a lot that week? Practices and games are jumping too. In season, cut the number of sets and keep the quality high instead of piling extra jumps on tired legs.

How long does it take to jump higher?

If you train consistently, you'll usually feel springier within a few weeks and see a measurable difference within about 6 to 8 weeks. Bigger jumps, the kind that change what you can do at the rim, come from stacking months of training.

How much you gain depends on your starting point, your age, how consistent you are, and how well you recover. Players who are new to training usually improve the fastest. Anyone promising a set number of inches in a set number of days is guessing.

6 mistakes that keep you stuck

  1. Only doing jumps. Without strength work, jump training stalls quickly.
  2. Doing too much. Hundreds of reps every day leads to tired legs and sore knees, not a higher vert. Quality beats volume.
  3. Skipping rest days. You get stronger while you recover, not while you train. Teens need around 8 to 10 hours of sleep a night.
  4. Ignoring landings. Land soft, with your knees tracking over your toes. Good landings keep you healthy enough to keep training.
  5. Testing every day. Max out every few weeks, not every session.
  6. Random workouts. Jumping between different videos every week means you never build on anything. Follow a plan.

How to measure your vertical

You don't need special equipment. Stand next to a wall, reach up as high as you can with one hand, and mark the spot. That's your standing reach. Then jump and touch as high as you can, and mark that. The difference is your vertical.

Test the same way every time (same shoes, same warm-up, standing or approach), and retest every 3 to 4 weeks. Write it down so you can see your progress.

Quick answers

Can you increase your vertical at any age?

Yes. Players of any age can improve their strength, power and technique. Teens often improve quickly because they're also growing and are new to training.

Do I need a gym?

A gym helps for strength work, but you can start at home with bodyweight squats, split squats, single-leg work, pogo hops, skips and broad jumps. Add weight once bodyweight gets easy.

Will lifting weights stunt my growth?

No. That's an old myth. Supervised, properly progressed strength training is considered safe for young athletes. The real risks come from bad form and too much weight too soon, which is why technique comes first.

How often should I train for vertical?

Three to four focused sessions a week, with rest days in between, works well for most players. More isn't better if you can't recover from it.

Results vary from athlete to athlete and depend on training experience, consistency, recovery and nutrition. Stop any exercise that causes sharp or lasting pain, and check with a qualified coach or healthcare professional before starting a new training program, especially if you're under 18 or coming back from an injury.
